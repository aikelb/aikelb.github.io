Between PlayMedusa and Aurita Games I've shipped over 80 titles. That number sounds impressive on a CV but honest? Most of them were small. Mobile hyper-casual games, client work, prototypes that turned into products. A few stood out.

What I learned across that volume isn't what you'd expect. It's not about engines or patterns, because those change every few years anyway. It's about finishing things. Shipping a mediocre game teaches you more than planning a great one. It's about knowing when to cut a feature instead of polishing it for another month. It's about reading a client's email and understanding what they actually need, not what they asked for.

The games I'm most proud of aren't the biggest ones. They're the ones where the team was small, the scope was realistic, and we actually shipped on time. *An American Werewolf in L.A.* made it to Switch. A Barbie mobile game I helped finish at Relativity crossed 10 million downloads, though honestly that one felt mostly like being in the right place at the right time.

If you're early in your career and worried about not having a hit: don't be. Ship things. Finish them. The volume compounds in ways you don't notice until you look back.

---

After 14 years making games, I took a job building AI agents.

It wasn't a dramatic pivot. Game development and AI work overlap more than you'd think. They both involve managing complexity, designing systems that respond to user input, and dealing with unpredictable edge cases. The main difference is that in games you're building worlds for players. With AI agents you're building tools for other developers.

I still write Python every day. I still think about user experience, just in a different context. The stakes feel lower in a good way. If the agent hallucinates a function name, nobody leaves a Steam review.

The hardest part was admitting I was ready for something new. I'd built my identity around being a game developer. Letting that go, even partially, felt like a loss. But I still tinker with Unity on weekends and keep up with what friends are shipping. The skills transfer. The community doesn't disappear. You just add a new chapter.

If you're considering a similar move: the technical transition is manageable. The identity shift is the real work.
